[[108]] Estimator memory on SEED. The five estimators compared in Section 4.2 were rerun on SEED under one protocol - same warm-up block, same scored trials, same classifier. The completed-block mean gains 0.214 (42 of 45 cells positive), the cumulative mean 0.214 and the variance-ratio rule 0.216, while the 50-trial window loses 0.041 and the exponential average 0.039. The gain therefore does not come from correction as such: it needs an estimate with long memory, and the two short-memory rules remove it entirely. On sleep-EDF the same five rules all lose (Section 4.7). The estimator and the task therefore interact, and the paper reports the interaction rather than a ranking of estimators.

[[109]] Memory scale. The estimator comparison suggests that the relevant quantity is the memory of the estimate relative to the duration of a class episode, so the memory was swept with everything else fixed. On SEED, averaging over the last 5 scored segments gives -0.113, over 10 -0.108, 25 -0.080, 50 -0.041, 100 +0.020, 200 +0.107 and 400 +0.203; an exponential average moves the same way with its rate (-0.105 at alpha = 0.2, +0.130 at alpha = 0.01, with the exponential average primed on the warm-up block so that all estimators start from the same history). Subject-level intervals: -0.113 [-0.145, -0.081] at 5 segments, +0.020 [-0.015, +0.055] at 100 and +0.203 [+0.164, +0.244] at 400, with 1, 8 and 15 of 15 subjects positive. The within-subject slope of the effect against log window is positive in all 15 subjects (mean +0.071 per log unit). The effect is monotone in memory and changes sign between 50 and 100 s, while a SEED film lasts 235 s (median run of the label sequence, identical across sessions): the crossover sits at about a quarter to a half of a film, as the memory-scale account predicts. The account is not extended to sleep-EDF: there the loss follows the class-mix part of the displacement, and no stage-duration statistic is used to predict what a memory sweep would do. Whether the two cases share a mechanism is left open rather than asserted.

[[134]] Where the sleep loss comes from. The displacement of the target mean splits exactly into a class-mix part (shared-class prior plus the classes present on only one side) and a within-class part, and the split can be applied without touching the task: the classifier is the one trained on the full source series, the scored trials are the original ones, and only the displacement that is removed changes (reconstruction error 2.1e-13 over all 4,983 cells). Removing the whole displacement costs 0.098; removing only the joint class-mix part costs 0.091 (32 of 32 subjects negative); removing only the shared-class prior alone costs 0.120; and removing only the class-conditional component gains 0.034, with 1 of 32 subjects negative. The joint test matters because the two mix components nearly cancel - projected norms 113.5 and 115.4 against a total of 13.6, with the conditional term at 5.7 - so a prior-only correction would have been a much larger perturbation than the real one. The joint term reproduces the loss, which is what the class-mix reading predicts; on the 693 cells whose class sets are identical the same four numbers are -0.051, -0.049, -0.049 and +0.031. The analysis locates the harm; it does not explain why the proportions move, and it uses target labels and a single global displacement, so it is a diagnostic, not an online method. The two components do not add: on the same cells the non-additive difference, defined as the full displacement's effect minus the sum of the two component effects, is -0.041 ([-0.048, -0.034]), negative in 31 of 32 subjects. Balanced accuracy is a nonlinear functional of the scores, so the two shifts do not act independently - removing the conditional part alone helps, yet removing it alongside the mix part does not protect against the loss.

[[154]] The cause is identified only in part. On the original task the decomposition of Section 4.7 shows that the harm travels with the joint class-mix part of the displacement: removing the within-class component alone gains 0.034, removing the joint class-mix part loses 0.091, and removing the shared-class prior alone loses 0.120 - the larger prior-only number is a perturbation the real displacement does not contain. On SEED the sign is monotone in the memory of the estimate and changes between 50 and 100 s, while films last two to four minutes; the same crossover does not occur on sleep-EDF, where every estimator loses. Because the mix components are an order of magnitude larger than their residual, the analysis says which part carries the loss, not why the proportions move. As for a label-free rule, none of the rules tested here - the gate fitted on one half of the subjects, the amplitude and dispersion statistics, or the sign models - held up across tasks: the gate avoids the sleep loss only by refusing to correct, and then discards the entire SEED gain. We state this as the result of the rules we tested, not as a claim that no such rule can exist. The model here is explanatory, not general: a symmetric equal-variance Gaussian with a linear threshold explains why a composition-only correction must hurt and how much drift is needed to reverse it, and the certificate is an identity for a fixed affine decoder. Neither is offered as a theory of domain adaptation, and the paper does not claim that the sign of the correction is predictable in general.
